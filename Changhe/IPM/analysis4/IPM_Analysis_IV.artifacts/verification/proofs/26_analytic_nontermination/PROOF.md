# Analytic S1 symmetry forbids finite termination

Status: CONFIRMED after independent adversarial review, 2026-10-31T03:52:43Z. See V049 and topic43 for the exact scope and evidence.

## Statement

Fix `c>1`, `delta=1-1/c`, and `p_n=1-n delta`. Suppose a stationary pure expansion has only finitely many density and stream terms:

\[
 \rho=\sum_{n=1}^N r^{p_n}f_n(\theta),\qquad
 \psi=\sum_{n=1}^M r^{p_n+1}g_n(\theta).
\]

Assume every angular coefficient is real analytic on the circle, with density symmetry `f_n(-theta)=-f_n(theta)`, `f_n(pi-theta)=f_n(theta)`, and stream symmetry `g_n(-theta)=-g_n(theta)`, `g_n(pi-theta)=-g_n(theta)`. Suppose `f_N` and `g_M` are nonzero, the Poisson relation `-Delta psi=partial_x rho` holds, and the full stationary equation holds on an exterior domain. These assumptions are inconsistent. In particular, allowing finitely many later density-free stream multipoles cannot restore finite termination.

Consequently, the analytic-data convergent solutions in topic 14, whose streams are generated by the pure nonresonant inverse, cannot terminate at any finite order when the leading density is nonzero. This proves that they are nontrivial infinite expansions, even when the input has infinitely many angular harmonics.

## A vanishing-order lemma

Let `f,g` be nonzero real analytic circle functions with the respective density and stream symmetries above. For real constants `a,b`, not both zero, the identity

\[
 a g f'-b g'f=0
\tag{1}
\]

is impossible.

To prove this, first expand at `theta=0`. Both functions are odd, so their nonzero vanishing orders `s,t` are positive odd integers. The leading coefficient of (1) is a nonzero constant times `a s-b t`. Hence

\[
 a s=b t.
\tag{2}
\]

If either `a` or `b` vanishes, (2) makes the other vanish, contrary to the assumption. Both are therefore nonzero.

At `theta=pi/2`, the function `f` is even about the center, so its vanishing order `s'` is a nonnegative even integer, including zero if its value there is nonzero. The function `g` is odd about the center, so its vanishing order `t'` is a positive odd integer. Analyticity and nontriviality make these orders finite. The leading coefficient of (1) gives

\[
 a s'=b t'.
\tag{3}
\]

For `s'=0` this is interpreted directly: the term `a g f'` has higher order, while `-b g'f` has a nonzero leading coefficient. Thus (3) would force `b=0`; this case is impossible. For `s'>0`, combine (2) and (3) to obtain `t s'=t' s`. The left side is even and the right side is odd. This contradiction proves the lemma.

## Applying the lemma to the last coefficient

The stream coefficient at density order `N` is nonzero, so `M>=N`. Otherwise the Poisson equation gives

\[
 p_N\cos\theta\, f_N-\sin\theta\, f_N'=0.
\]

At zero, let `s>=1` be the odd vanishing order of `f_N`. The first nonzero Taylor coefficient of this expression is proportional to `p_N-s`. Since `p_N<=p_1=1-delta<1`, it cannot equal `s`. This contradiction shows that `g_N` is nonzero.

In the stationary equation, the nonlinear term of order `M+N` has coefficient

\[
 (p_M+1)g_M f_N'-p_N g_M'f_N.
\tag{4}
\]

There is no linear term of that order and no other pair with stream index at most `M` and density index at most `N` adding to `M+N`. Distinct radial powers are linearly independent on an exterior interval, so (4) must vanish identically.

Apply the lemma with `a=p_M+1` and `b=p_N`. These constants cannot both be zero. For `M=N` their difference is one. For `M>N`, the coefficient `g_M` has zero density forcing and is a nonzero periodic odd harmonic mode; if `p_M+1=0`, its harmonic equation would be `g_M''=0`, forcing it to vanish. Thus `a` is nonzero in that case. The lemma says that (4) cannot vanish, completing the contradiction.

## Extension to arbitrary finite pure powers

The same argument permits any finite sets of real radial powers, rather than just the prescribed exponent lattice. More precisely, let

\[
 \rho=\sum_{\ell=1}^N r^{s_\ell}f_\ell(\theta),\qquad
 \psi=\sum_{j=1}^M r^{q_j}g_j(\theta),
\]

where the exponents in each sum are distinct, the coefficients are nonzero and analytic with the same symmetries, and the smallest density exponent \(s_*=\min_\ell s_\ell\) satisfies \(s_*<1\). Suppose the Poisson relation and stationary equation hold. These assumptions are inconsistent.

Indeed, the derivative \(\partial_x(r^{s_*}f_*)\) is nonzero by the vanishing-order argument above. Matching the radial power in the Poisson equation therefore requires a stream term with exponent \(s_*+1\). If \(q_*=\min_j q_j\), this gives \(q_*\le s_*+1\). The smallest nonlinear radial exponent is uniquely

\[
 q_*+s_*-2\le2s_*-1<s_*.
\]

There is no linear density term with this exponent. Its angular coefficient is \(q_*g_*f_*'-s_*g_*'f_*\), which must vanish. The vanishing-order lemma rules this out unless \(q_*=s_*=0\). In that exceptional pair, all density exponents are nonnegative, so none can force the Laplacian of the stream term at exponent zero: that would require a density exponent \(-1\). Consequently \(g_*''=0\), contradicting the nonzero periodic odd coefficient. This proves the extension.

The condition on the smallest exponent suffices; in the far-field problem every density exponent is already below one.

## Dependencies and scope

The only dependencies are the independently derived stationary and Poisson identities, the stated S1 symmetries, Taylor expansions of nonzero analytic functions, and independence of finitely many distinct real powers. No computation or proof assistant is needed.

The proof allows finitely many density-free harmonic stream terms and the extension allows arbitrary finite pure radial powers. It excludes logarithmic channels. It does not apply to merely smooth coefficients that can vanish to infinite order at the axes, and it does not establish divergence of an infinite series.
