# Why a weight depending only on log deficit does not close multiplication

Status: CONFIRMED after independent theory-part review and verifier recheck at 2026-10-31T05:42:01Z. This rules out one proposed absolute majorant norm. It is not a divergence theorem for the IPM recurrence.

## Statement

Let R>0 and let (w_d)_(d>=0) be any sequence of finite strictly positive real numbers. On the scalar polynomials

    A(z,t)=sum_(k>=1) sum_(0<=q<=k-1) a_(k,q) z^k t^q

put d=k-1-q and define

    ||A||_(R,w)=sum_(k,q) R^k w_(k-1-q) q! |a_(k,q)|.       (1)

There is no finite constant C for which

    ||AB||_(R,w) <= C ||A||_(R,w) ||B||_(R,w)               (2)

holds for all such polynomials. The conclusion also holds for any fixed polynomial damping factor in the output block index, or for norms in which (1) is additionally multiplied by a fixed power of k.

In the rational IPM block convention, d is the log deficit and q! |a| is the factorial-normalized column size. Thus a fixed positive weight depending only on d cannot turn the coefficientwise multiplication estimate into a bounded operation at the same radial radius. A norm with additional order-dependent weights, a radius loss, or an estimate retaining the signed structure of the actual recurrence is not ruled out.

## Proof

For each integer m>=1, take

    A_m(z,t)=B_m(z,t)=z^m t^(m-1)/(m-1)!.

Both inputs have deficit0 and norm R^m w_0. Their product has block index2m, log degree2m-2 and deficit1. Its norm is

    ||A_m B_m||_(R,w)
        =R^(2m) w_1 (2m-2)!/((m-1)!)^2
        =R^(2m) w_1 binom(2m-2,m-1).                      (3)

The ratio in (2) is therefore exactly

    (w_1/w_0^2) binom(2m-2,m-1).                          (4)

Writing h=m-1, the sum of the2h+1 binomial coefficients in row2h is4^h, and the middle coefficient is the largest. Hence

    binom(2h,h) >= 4^h/(2h+1).                            (5)

This tends to infinity and proves the assertion for every choice of the positive weights. Multiplying (4) by any nonzero fixed power of m, or dividing it by such a power, still leaves exponential growth. This proves the statement about polynomial order factors.

The IPM block product can shift the output block by one. Such a fixed shift changes R^(2m) by a fixed factor and replaces w_1 by w_2, while multiplying the factorial ratio by a fixed-degree rational function of m. Since every fixed deficit weight is strictly positive, the same obstruction remains.

## A quantitative radius-loss consequence

Suppose inputs are measured at radius R and outputs at a smaller radius r. The same ratio becomes

    (w_1/w_0^2)(r/R)^(2m) binom(2m-2,m-1).                (6)

If2r>R, (5) makes this unbounded. Thus a bilinear bound of this form requires r<=R/2. This is only a necessary condition. It is not a proof that a half-radius loss suffices for all coefficients or for the IPM operators.

The factor2 matches the fixed-deficit forcing estimate: two columns with exponential rates b_a and b_b naturally give rate b_a+b_b. A deficit weight changes a constant but cannot change that exponential rate.

## Scope and relation to the recurrence

The counterexample uses arbitrary scalar coefficient arrays. It does not show that the arrays generated by the IPM recursion realize these extremizers. The actual Jacobian combines signed angular products, the Poisson map, and the stationary inverse; these may provide cancellations or extra relations unavailable to a coefficientwise absolute product bound. Nor does the argument rule out a norm with weights depending jointly on block index and deficit.

The result addresses the exact proposed step "choose positive weights w_d so that the factorial-column convolution is bounded at the same radial radius." That step is impossible without further structure. Full convergence of the unprojected rational logarithmic series remains OPEN.

## Verification

The proof is an exact monomial computation and the binomial theorem. A separate rational-polynomial calculation checks the norm ratios and the lower bound for a finite range of m; these checks support the implementation of the formula and are not the all-order proof.
