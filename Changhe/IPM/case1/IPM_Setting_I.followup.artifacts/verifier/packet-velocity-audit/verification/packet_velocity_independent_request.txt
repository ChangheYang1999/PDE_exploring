# Full-vector velocity and the constant gauge of frozen packets

Date: 2026-10-20. This is a standalone audit of the velocity used in the frozen packet equation. It makes no assertion about a global coupled profile.

## Statement, hypotheses and conventions

Let A>1, g>0, q=2(A-1), b=g/q, c_F=(2pi)^-2, and Phi be a smooth complex function with compact support in R minus{0}, integral Phi=0 and Phi(-t)=-conjugate(Phi(t)). Let v be the continuous real frozen packet of ../packet-dyadic-audit/PROOF.md:

    v(s,y)=2i c_F integral_0^infinity r^-2 sin(yr)
           integral Phi(t)e^(i s t r^A)(1+t^2r^q)^(-b)dt dr.       (1)

Define two real functions

    U_2(s,y)=-2i c_F integral_0^infinity r^(q-2) sin(yr)
              integral t^2 Phi(t)e^(i s t r^A)
                                (1+t^2r^q)^(-b-1)dt dr,         (2)

    U_1(s,y)=2 c_F integral_0^infinity r^(A-3)
              integral t Phi(t)(1+t^2r^q)^(-b-1)
                              [e^(i s t r^A)cos(yr)-1]dt dr.    (3)

Then these grouped integrals converge locally uniformly, U is continuous, U(0,0)=0, U_1 is even in y and U_2 is odd in y. They satisfy the distributional equations on the whole plane

    d_s U_1+d_y U_2=0,
    d_s U_2-d_y U_1=-d_s v.                                    (4)

Both velocity components have strictly sublinear growth. Among all distributional solutions of(4) represented by continuous sublinear functions, U is unique after fixing U(0,0)=0. If only U_2 is required odd in y, the second component is unique and the remaining freedom is one constant horizontal component. No linear harmonic velocity can be added in this class.

When Phi=i phi with phi real and even, v is even in s; U_1 is odd in s and U_2 even in s. This is the full centered parity about(s,y)=(0,0), and removes both constant components without an extra normalization. This centered packet parity is not a statement of the original profile's parity about x=0 after translation to P.

For A>2, removing the subtraction -1 in(3) defines a continuous U_1^dec that tends to zero at spatial infinity. Formula(2) also tends to zero at infinity in this range. Thus U^dec is the unique sublinear div-curl solution that tends to zero. Its first component differs from(3) by a constant. For 1<A<=2 the bare unsubtracted integral can diverge at zero frequency; an anchoring prescription cannot be omitted without additional moment conditions. We give its exact cancellation criterion below.

The source equation A s v_s+y v_y-v+g U_2=0 is already proved for the same U_2 in the dependent packet proof. Our conclusion identifies that component by a whole-plane sublinear div-curl characterization. It does not assert that the original profile's spatial symmetric-truncation prescription applies to every translated packet.

## Convergence, reality and distributional identities

On the fixed t support, as r tends to zero the bracket in(3) is bounded on compact physical sets by C(r^A+r^2), by the mean-value bound for the exponential and the second-order cosine bound. Thus its absolute integrand after t integration is bounded by

    C(r^(2A-3)+r^(A-1)),

which is integrable for A>1. In(2), |sin(yr)|<=C r supplies the bound C r^(q-1), also integrable. At infinity the absolute bounds are C r^(-A-1-g) in(3), since q(b+1)=g+q, and C r^(-2-g) in(2). These estimates are locally uniform and prove continuity by dominated convergence. In(3), complex conjugation followed by t mapped to -t leaves the integral unchanged. The inner integral in(2) is purely imaginary under the same operation. Both U components are therefore real. The y parity and the normalization follow directly. For imaginary even Phi, t Phi is odd and the only surviving part of(3) is the sine in s, proving its s parity; the other assertions follow in the same way from the cosine in s.

First truncate all r integrals to epsilon<r<R. They are smooth, so differentiation and the following cancellation are legitimate. Write

    z=t^2r^q, f=(1+z)^(-b), f_1=(1+z)^(-b-1).

The divergence contributions from d_s U_1 and d_y U_2 are respectively plus and minus

    2i c_F r^(2A-3) integral t^2 Phi(t)e^(i s t r^A)cos(yr) f_1 dt,

because q-1=2A-3. Thus divergence is zero for every truncation. For the curl, d_s U_2 and -d_y U_1 add to

    2 c_F r^(A-2) sin(yr)
       integral t Phi(t)e^(i s t r^A)(z+1)f_1 dt,

which equals -d_s v's truncated integrand because(z+1)f_1=f. The subtracted constant in(3) differentiates to zero. The truncated v and U converge locally uniformly by the preceding estimates and the dependent density proof. Passing to distributional derivatives proves(4). This argument requires no unjustified pointwise differentiation of a high-frequency integral.

## Sublinear growth, with endpoint logarithms

The r>=1 parts of(2) and(3) are bounded uniformly in(s,y). Set S=|s| and Y=|y|. For0<r<1, using boundedness of the amplitude and the fixed t support gives

    |U_2| <= C + C integral_0^1 r^(q-2) min(1,Yr)dr,             (5)

    |U_1| <= C + C integral_0^1 r^(A-3)
                             [min(1,Sr^A)+min(1,Y^2r^2)]dr.    (6)

In(6) the estimate follows by writing e^(i s t r^A)cos(yr)-1 as(e^(i s t r^A)-1)cos(yr)+(cos(yr)-1). Constants absorb the harmless factor2. When S or Y is at most1, its term is uniformly bounded, so assume they exceed1.

Splitting(5) at r=Y^-1 gives a bounded function for q>1, a bound C(1+log Y) for q=1, and C Y^(1-q) for0<q<1. For the S term in(6), split at r=S^(-1/A). Direct integration gives C S^((2-A)/A) for1<A<2, C(1+log S) for A=2, and a constant for A>2. For the Y term split at r=Y^-1; it gives C Y^(2-A), C(1+log Y), or a constant in those respective ranges. Thus, in particular,

    |U_1(X)| <= C(1+|X|)^(2-A),                    1<A<2,
               C(1+log(2+|X|)),                   A=2,
               C,                                A>2.

Every exponent that occurs is less than1. A logarithm is bounded by C_delta(1+|X|)^delta for any fixed0<delta<1. Both components therefore satisfy a uniform bound C(1+|X|)^kappa for some kappa<1 depending only on A and the chosen logarithmic majorant.

## Uniqueness and removal of harmonic freedom

The difference H of two such velocities has zero divergence and curl. In distributions,

    Delta H_1=d_s div H-d_y curl H=0,
    Delta H_2=d_y div H+d_s curl H=0.

For completeness, one can apply this argument to H convolved with a compact smooth approximate identity. Each mollified component is a smooth entire harmonic function and has the same growth exponent kappa<1. The interior gradient estimate obtained by differentiating the Poisson formula on a ball of radius R gives

    |grad H_epsilon(X)| <= C/R sup_(B(X,R)) |H_epsilon|
                         <= C_X R^(kappa-1) ->0.

Hence each H_epsilon is constant. Letting epsilon tend to zero in distributions makes H constant, and continuity gives pointwise equality. Fixing H(0)=0 removes it. Alternatively, oddness of H_2 in y removes its vertical component and oddness of H_1 in s removes its horizontal component. A stream-function addition lambda s y would give(-lambda s,lambda y), which grows linearly, and so cannot occur. This is a growth-and-normalization statement, not a choice of a wall Green function.

## The unanchored integral and its low-frequency moments

For A>2 the frequency L^1 norm of the unanchored first component is finite. In the coordinates xi=t r^A, eta=plus or minus r, it is bounded at zero by C integral_0^1 r^(A-3)dr and at infinity by C integral_1^infinity r^(-A-1-g)dr. The second component is also frequency integrable in this range: its low-r absolute weight is r^(q-2), integrable because q>1. Their frequency functions are ordinary L^1 functions on R^2, so Fourier inversion and approximation in L^1 by smooth compactly supported frequency functions show that both inverse transforms tend to zero at infinity. For the latter assertion, integration by parts proves decay for each smooth compactly supported approximation, while its Fourier inverse error is bounded uniformly by its L^1 error. Formula(3) is the unanchored first component minus its value at zero.

Here is the precise radial criterion in the remaining range. Define

    m_k=integral t^(2k+1)Phi(t)dt,
    d_k=(-1)^k(b+1)(b+2)...(b+k)/k!, d_0=1.

For any K, uniformly on the fixed t support,

    integral t Phi(t)(1+t^2r^q)^(-b-1)dt
                      =sum_(k<K) d_k m_k r^(kq)+O(r^(Kq)).       (7)

Choose K so A-2+Kq>0. The unanchored radial integral at(0,0) converges as epsilon tends to zero exactly if

    m_k=0 for every integer k>=0 with A-2+kq<=0.                 (8)

Indeed every such k<K has a nonzero d_k, distinct real radial exponent A-3+kq<=-1, and gives respectively a distinct divergent power or a logarithm after integration. A nonzero first divergent coefficient cannot be cancelled by the other powers or a logarithm. The remainder is integrable by the choice of K. This proves necessity and sufficiency. At every other fixed physical point, the difference from the origin integrand is exactly the anchored bracket in(3), whose local uniform convergence was proved already. Thus(8) is also the convergence criterion for the bare grouped integral at all finite points. It does not assert absolute convergence before the t integration.

The zero density moment integral Phi=0 does not imply(8). For example a nonzero real odd Phi=t chi(t), with chi a nonnegative real even smooth function compactly supported away from zero, satisfies both density conditions but has m_0=integral t^2 chi(t)dt>0. Thus for every1<A<=2 its bare first-velocity integral diverges. In the imaginary even subspace all m_k vanish by oddness, and the unanchored and anchored first integrals coincide because their grouped origin integral is identically zero. The general anchored formula remains valid without those additional moments.

## Dependencies, verification and scope

Only the continuous density construction and frozen U_2 equation in ../packet-dyadic-audit/PROOF.md are used. The full-vector integral, estimates, distributional div-curl passage, sublinear uniqueness and radial moment criterion are proved here. These packets still fail the profile class's weighted gradient condition by that dependent proof. No candidate residual, full variable-coefficient inverse, kernel correction or endpoint follows. # Frozen packets: direct dyadic estimates and all normalized axis traces

Date: 2026-10-20. This is an independent proof of a specified part of the frozen-packet statement in the lead's section6.4. It does not prove the full asymptotic expansion or any correction to the variable-coefficient equation.

## Exact statement and definitions

Let A>1, g>0, q=2(A-1), b=g/q and h=1+g. Choose a smooth complex function Phi compactly supported in R minus{0}, satisfying

    integral Phi(t)dt=0,
    Phi(-t)=-conjugate(Phi(t)).

The Fourier transform convention is fhat(xi,eta)=integral exp(-i(s xi+y eta))f(s,y)dsdy, with inverse factor(2pi)^-2. Define the tempered distribution Vhat by the limit of the following formula restricted to |eta|>epsilon:

    Vhat(xi,eta)=sgn(eta)|eta|^(-A-2)
       (1+xi^2/eta^2)^(-b) Phi(xi/|eta|^A).

The extension at zero frequency is part of this definition. Let v be its inverse transform. Define U_2[v] by the same limiting convention after multiplying the displayed expression by -xi^2/(xi^2+eta^2). Conclusions:

1. These limits exist. The density is the real continuous function

    v(s,y)=2i/(2pi)^2 integral_0^infinity sin(yr)r^-2
        integral Phi(t)exp(i s t r^A)(1+t^2 r^q)^(-b)dt dr.       (1)

   It is odd in y and satisfies the frozen equation

    A s v_s+y v_y-v+g U_2[v]=0                                (2)

   distributionally, with no additional distribution at zero frequency.
2. v is smooth off(0,0). If m is a nonnegative integer, 0<alpha<1 and(m+alpha)A<=h, then v belongs to C^(m,alpha)_loc. The noninteger endpoint in this statement is included.
3. For every integer j>=2, the trace d_y^j v(s,0) is smooth for s!=0 and satisfies

    |d_y^j v(s,0)| <= C_j times
       1,                         j<h,
       1+log(1/|s|),              j=h,
       1+|s|^((h-j)/A),           j>h,                         (3)

   for0<|s|<=1/2. Thus, for every odd j>=3,

    |s|^((j-1)/A) d_y^j v(s,0)/j! ->0.                     (4)

   Neither boundedness of all unweighted traces nor absence of smooth Taylor terms is asserted.
4. The mapping Phi to v is injective. If h/A>3, it gives an infinite-dimensional real space of frozen solutions in C^(2,alpha)_loc for every alpha<1, smooth off the source and satisfying(4) at every odd order.
5. Every such packet satisfies |v(X)|<=C(1+|X|)^(1/A). Every nonzero packet fails the global bound |grad v(X)|<=C(1+|X|)^(gamma-1) for every gamma<1. Hence these uncut packets do not satisfy that growth condition of the profile class.

There is no assertion that they solve the nonlinear or variable-coefficient IPM equation, that their cutoffs correct to kernel elements, or that they yield a family or either task endpoint. The full quasi-homogeneous expansion with possible resonant logarithms remains outside this proof.

## The distribution at low frequency and its equation

Set xi=t r^A, eta=plus or minus r. The Jacobian is r^A. For a Schwartz test function f, the pairing becomes

    integral_epsilon^infinity r^-2 integral Phi(t)(1+t^2r^q)^(-b)
           [f(t r^A,r)-f(t r^A,-r)] dt dr.                   (5)

For r<=1 and t in the fixed support,

    f(t r^A,r)-f(t r^A,-r)
       =2r f_eta(0,0)+O(r^(A+1)+r^3).

The amplitude is1+O(r^q), uniformly with t derivatives on this support. The zero moment of Phi cancels the leading term. The absolute value after the t integration is at most a fixed Schwartz seminorm times

    C(r^(q-1)+r^(A-1)+r),

which is integrable since A>1 and q>0. At infinity, the test function gives arbitrary polynomial decay and the integral converges. The same argument proves continuity with respect to finitely many Schwartz seminorms, hence defines a tempered distribution. Multiplication by the second-velocity symbol adds a factor O(r^q) at zero, so its pairing converges too.

Let D=A xi d_xi+eta d_eta. The quantity t=xi/|eta|^A is invariant under D and D r=r. Direct differentiation away from eta=0 gives

    D Vhat=(-A-2-g xi^2/(xi^2+eta^2))Vhat.

The Fourier transform of(2) is therefore zero away from eta=0. On the cutoff |eta|>epsilon, integration by parts leaves only the boundary from D crossing r=epsilon. Explicitly, D 1_(|eta|>epsilon)=|eta| delta(|eta|-epsilon). The factor sgn(eta) turns the two boundary lines into the difference in(5). Its pairing is epsilon times the radial integrand in(5), hence O(epsilon^q+epsilon^A+epsilon^2) after the same cancellation. It tends to zero. The support in t is away from zero and compact, so there is no separate boundary on xi=0 or at t infinity. This proves(2) as a distribution on the whole plane.

Grouping positive and negative eta in Fourier inversion gives(1). On bounded sets of(s,y), its low-r integrand is bounded by C(r^(A-1)+r^(q-1)): subtract the zero moment in the inner integral and use |sin(yr)|<=C r. For r>=1, the integrand is bounded by C r^(-2-g), since t stays away from zero. Both bounds are integrable and locally uniform, proving continuity and agreement with the distributional inverse. The reality condition makes the inner t integral purely imaginary for real s,r; thus v is real. Oddness in y follows from the sine. These statements also hold for U_2 with its inserted multiplier.

## Smooth low frequencies and uniformly Schwartz dyadic pieces

Choose a smooth partition of unity on r>0 consisting of one term supported in r<2 and terms chi(2^-n r), n>=0, with chi supported in a fixed compact subinterval of(0,infinity). Overlap is uniformly finite. The low-frequency part is smooth in(s,y). To check every derivative at r=0, write a for the number of s derivatives and c for y derivatives. If a>=1, the inner integral gains r^(Aa). For c=0 the sine gives an extra r; for c>=1 the derivative supplies r^c. All resulting powers are integrable since A>1. If a=0,c=0 or1, use the zero-moment cancellation O(r^A+r^q); if c>=2 the factor r^c alone suffices. This also justifies derivatives uniformly on compact sets.

For the n-th high-frequency piece substitute r=2^n u. Since qb=g, it has the exact form

    v_n(s,y)=2^(-hn) H_n(2^(An)s,2^n y).                  (6)

The inverse Fourier integrand defining H_n is supported where u lies in the fixed support of chi and t lies in the fixed support of Phi. On each sign of eta, the map(t,u) to(t u^A,plus or minus u) is a smooth diffeomorphism with nonzero Jacobian. Its amplitude includes

    (2^(-qn)+t^2u^q)^(-b),

whose derivatives of every fixed order are bounded uniformly for n>=0. This follows because |t| and u have fixed positive lower bounds. All other factors are fixed smooth functions on this compact support. Integration by parts in the two Fourier variables, with no boundary terms because the cutoff functions are smooth and compactly supported, proves H_n are uniformly Schwartz. Consequently, for every a,c,N>=0,

    |d_s^a d_y^c v_n(s,y)|
       <= C_(a,c,N) 2^((Aa+c-h)n)
          (1+2^(An)|s|+2^n|y|)^(-N).                  (7)

On any compact set separated from(0,0), choosing N sufficiently large makes the derivative series uniformly summable. Thus v is smooth there.

## Ordinary Holder regularity, including the noninteger endpoint

For a derivative of total order m, Aa+c<=mA. Its uniform norm at scale n is bounded by C 2^((mA-h)n). Its first derivative is bounded by C 2^(((m+1)A-h)n). The mean-value inequality and the uniform bound give, for delta=|X-X'|<=1,

    |D^m v_n(X)-D^m v_n(X')|
       <= C 2^((mA-h)n) min(1,2^(An)delta).             (8)

Put theta=h/A-m. The assumption gives theta>=alpha>0. If0<theta<1, splitting at2^(An)delta=1 sums two geometric series and gives C delta^theta. If theta=1, the low-scale part is at most C delta(1+log(1/delta)), bounded by C_alpha delta^alpha for every alpha<1. If theta>1, the derivative series of order m+1 is summable and gives a Lipschitz bound. Thus in every permitted case(8) sums to C delta^alpha. The derivatives of order m themselves are uniformly summable because theta>0; the smooth low-frequency term causes no difficulty on a compact set. This proves the asserted C^(m,alpha)_loc regularity without assuming any asymptotic expansion.

## Axis traces at every order

At y=0, inequality(7) gives

    |d_y^j v_n(s,0)|<=C_(j,N)2^((j-h)n)(1+2^(An)|s|)^(-N).

For0<|s|<=1/2 split the series at2^(An)|s|=1. Before the split, it is a bounded geometric series if j<h, a sum of O(1+log(1/|s|)) constants if j=h, and at most C|s|^((h-j)/A) if j>h. After the split, take AN>max(j-h,0); the geometric tail gives the same respective bound, with a bounded contribution from the smooth low frequencies. This proves(3). Multiplying by |s|^((j-1)/A) gives a vanishing bound: its exponent is(j-1)/A when j<h, with a logarithm if j=h, and g/A>0 when j>h. The conclusion(4) follows for every stated j. The constant may depend on j; no uniform all-orders estimate is claimed.

## Injectivity, dimension and far-field admissibility

If v is zero as a distribution, Vhat is zero. On eta>0 away from the origin its formula is a smooth function, with nonvanishing scalar factors multiplying Phi. Thus Phi is zero. The reality and moment conditions leave an infinite-dimensional real space: take Phi=i f, where f is any real even smooth compactly supported function away from zero with integral zero. Injectivity and the regularity and trace estimates prove conclusion4.

For growth, put S=|s| and Y=|y|. On0<r<1 subtract the zero moment to bound the inner integral in(1) by

    C(min(1,S r^A)+r^q).

The first term contributes at most C(1+S)^(1/A), even after replacing the sine by1, by splitting at r=S^(-1/A) when S>1. The second contributes at most

    C integral_0^1 min(1,Yr) r^(q-2)dr.

For Y>1, split this integral at r=1/Y; for Y<=1 its bound is constant. For q<1 it is bounded by C(1+Y)^(1-q), for q=1 by C(1+log(1+Y)), and for q>1 by a constant. Since1-q=3-2A<1/A for A>1, all cases are bounded by C(1+Y)^(1/A). The high-r part is uniformly bounded. This proves the isotropic growth bound.

A stronger fact follows from rescaling(1): as R tends to infinity,

    R^-1 v(R^A s,R y) -> V_0(s,y),                      (9)

locally uniformly, where V_0 is(1) with the amplitude(1+t^2r^q)^(-b) replaced by1. For the rescaled integral, the amplitude is(1+t^2r^q/R^q)^(-b). At r<=1 the same moment cancellation gives an integrable bound C(r^(A-1)+r^(q-1)), uniform for R>=1 and compact(s,y); at r>=1 use C r^-2. Dominated convergence, with the same bounds on compact sets, proves(9). The limiting Fourier distribution is given by the preceding construction with amplitude1: the same zero-moment cancellation gives a convergent low-frequency pairing, and the locally uniform integral bounds give its continuous inverse V_0. It is nonzero for nonzero Phi, again by its formula on eta>0. V_0 is odd in y.

Suppose the weighted gradient bound in conclusion5 held. On each compact set with |s| bounded away from zero,

    d_y[R^-1 v(R^A s,Ry)]=v_y(R^A s,Ry)
       =O(R^(A(gamma-1))) ->0.

Using the fundamental theorem of calculus between two finite y values and the locally uniform limit(9), V_0 is independent of y on s!=0. Oddness makes it zero there, and continuity makes it zero on s=0 as well. This contradicts its nonzero Fourier distribution. Thus no nonzero packet satisfies that weighted gradient condition. This is a failure of one admissibility condition for the uncut field, not a conclusion about a compact cutoff or its possible correction.

The needed estimates are supplied above. It does not use numerics, the full quasi-homogeneous expansion, a classification of all frozen solutions, or a variable-coefficient inverse. In particular it supplies no exact coupled profile, no smooth-center candidate and no endpoint. Review evidence and any finite exponent checks belong in verification/.
