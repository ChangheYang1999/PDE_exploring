# Pressure normalization forced by decaying velocity

Status: CONFIRMED in A031 after independent literature-agent challenge and a second auditor check. This result fixes the pressure gradient; it does not establish pointwise pressure decay. The challenge is archived in the team records.

## Statement

Let `c,omega` be finite real constants. Let `U in C^1(R^3;R^3)` and `P in C^1(R^3)` solve

\[
 (1-c)U+((cI-\omega J)y+U)\cdot\nabla U+\omega JU+\nabla P=0,
 \qquad \operatorname{div}U=0.                              \tag{1}
\]

Assume `U` is bounded and `U(Rz)->0` in distributions on compact sets as `R->infinity`. In particular, this assumption holds if `U(y)->0` uniformly at infinity, or if `U` is bounded and belongs to `L^2(R^3)`.

Put `F_ij=U_iU_j`. The pressure gradient in (1) equals the canonical double-Riesz pressure gradient generated by `F`. Equivalently, pressure is the canonical distributional pressure modulo a constant; no nonconstant harmonic pressure term is allowed.

As a consequence, if additionally `U in L^2(R^3)` and `c != 2/5`, then `U=0` and `P` is constant. No asymptotic pressure assumption is needed in this bounded finite-energy corollary.

## Notation and definition of the canonical gradient

Let `T_ij=R_i R_j` be the Fourier multiplier

\[
 \widehat{T_{ij}\psi}(\xi)
       =-\frac{\xi_i\xi_j}{|\xi|^2}\widehat\psi(\xi),
 \qquad \xi\ne0.
\]

For every Schwartz function `psi` of integral zero, `T_ij psi` belongs to `L^1`. Its distributional kernel is

\[
 -\tfrac13\delta_{ij}\delta_0+\operatorname{p.v.}
 \frac{3x_ix_j-\delta_{ij}|x|^2}{4\pi|x|^5}.
\]

Here is the needed integrability check. The principal-value kernel is homogeneous of degree `-3` and smooth off the origin. At large distance, its leading term against `psi` is the kernel times `integral psi`, which vanishes. Subtracting that term and using one derivative of the kernel leaves `O(|x|^-4)`, with bounds controlled by Schwartz seminorms; the Schwartz tails are estimated by splitting the convolution into `|z|<|x|/2` and its complement. Locally the principal-value cancellation and smoothness of `psi` give a bounded function. This proves integrability and continuity from the zero-mean Schwartz subspace into `L^1`.

Define the canonical pressure functional on this subspace by

\[
 \langle P_{\rm can},\psi\rangle
       =\sum_{i,j}\int F_{ij}(x)\,T_{ij}\psi(x)\,dx.          \tag{2}
\]

Boundedness of `F` makes these integrals absolutely convergent. To extend this to a tempered distribution, fix any Schwartz `rho` with integral one and replace an arbitrary test `psi` by `psi-(integral psi)rho` in (2). Different extensions differ only by a constant distribution. Thus the gradient is unambiguous:

\[
 \langle\partial_kP_{\rm can},\phi\rangle
       =-\sum_{i,j}\int F_{ij}(x)\,T_{ij}\partial_k\phi(x)\,dx.
                                                               \tag{3}
\]

The proof below is about this well-defined gradient. It does not need an `L^infinity` estimate for a Riesz transform, which would be false.

## 1. Poisson equation and the harmonic remainder

Distributional divergence of (1), using `div U=0` and cancellation of the two rotation terms, gives

\[
 -\Delta P=\partial_i\partial_jF_{ij}.                         \tag{4}
\]

The multiplier identity `T_ij(-Delta phi)=partial_i partial_j phi` and (2) show that `P_can` satisfies the same Poisson equation. Hence `Q=P-P_can` is a harmonic distribution.

Write `A=cI-omega J`. The momentum equation in divergence form is

\[
 \partial_k P
   =-(1-4c)U_k-\omega(JU)_k
        -\partial_j\big((Ay)_jU_k+U_jU_k\big).                \tag{5}
\]

Every term on the right is tempered because `U` is bounded and `Ay` grows linearly. Thus each component of `grad P` is tempered. Formula (3) gives the same conclusion for `grad P_can`, so each `partial_k Q` is a tempered harmonic distribution.

Every tempered harmonic distribution is a harmonic polynomial: after Fourier transform its support is contained in `{0}`, because multiplication by `|xi|^2` annihilates it. A distribution supported at one point is a finite linear combination of derivatives of the delta distribution; its inverse Fourier transform is a polynomial. Therefore each component of `grad Q` is a polynomial.

This argument does not presume that the original pressure itself has polynomial growth.

## 2. Large-scale testing removes that polynomial

Set `U_R(z)=U(Rz)`. Equation (1), tested in the `z` variable, reads

\[
 \nabla_yP(Rz)
 =-(1-c)U_R-\omega JU_R
      -(Az\cdot\nabla_z)U_R
      -R^{-1}\operatorname{div}_z(U_R\otimes U_R).             \tag{6}
\]

The first three terms tend to zero in distributions on compact sets by the assumed distributional convergence of `U_R`. This includes the linear transport term, since distributional differentiation and multiplication by a fixed smooth coefficient are continuous. The final term tends to zero because `U_R` is uniformly bounded and has the prefactor `R^-1`. Hence the physical gradient `grad_y P(Rz)` tends to zero in distributions in the variable `z`.

For the canonical gradient, homogeneity of the degree-zero multiplier and (3) give, for any compactly supported smooth scalar `phi`,

\[
 \langle(\partial_kP_{\rm can})(R\cdot),\phi\rangle
 =-R^{-1}\sum_{i,j}\int F_{ij}(Rz)\,
                              T_{ij}\partial_k\phi(z)\,dz.    \tag{7}
\]

Because `T_ij partial_k phi` is integrable, the absolute value is at most `C_phi ||U||_infinity^2/R`. In particular this gradient also tends to zero in distributions under dilation.

Subtracting (7) from the limit of (6) shows that the polynomial `grad Q(Rz)` tends to zero as a distribution. A nonzero polynomial cannot have that property. For example, test against a compactly supported smooth function whose moments isolate a nonzero coefficient of maximal degree; its pairing grows like a nonzero constant times that power of `R`. A nonzero constant polynomial also fails to tend to zero. Therefore `grad Q=0`, proving

\[
 \nabla P=\nabla P_{\rm can}.                                 \tag{8}
\]

The scaling hypothesis holds for decaying `U` by dominated convergence, since `U(Rz)->0` for every `z!=0`. It holds for bounded `L^2` velocity because `||U(Rz)||_{L^2(K,dz)}<=R^{-3/2}||U||_2` for every fixed compact `K`.

## 3. Bounded finite-energy corollary

If `U` is bounded and in `L^2`, then `F_ij in L^2`. The bounded Fourier multiplier defines

\[
 P_2=\sum_{i,j}T_{ij}F_{ij}\in L^2.
\]

By duality, its gradient is exactly (3). Thus (8) yields `P=P_2+constant`.

Use a fixed smooth radial cutoff `phi_R(y)=phi(y/R)`, equal to one on the unit ball before scaling and zero outside the ball of radius two. Set `q=|U|^2/2`. The localized energy identity is

\[
 (2-5c)\int q\phi_R
   =\int(qB+P_2U)\cdot\nabla\phi_R.                           \tag{9}
\]

The constant pressure contributes zero because `div U=0`. Rotation contributes zero because the cutoff is radial. The remaining linear drift contribution is bounded by

\[
 C|c|\int_{R<|y|<2R}|U|^2\longrightarrow0.
\]

The cubic term is bounded by

\[
 C R^{-1}\|U\|_\infty\int_{R<|y|<2R}|U|^2\longrightarrow0,
\]

and the pressure term by

\[
 C R^{-1}\|P_2\|_2\|U\|_2\longrightarrow0.
\]

Dominated convergence on the left proves

\[
 (1-\tfrac52c)\int|U|^2=0.
\]

For `c!=2/5`, `U=0`, and the momentum equation makes pressure constant.

## Scope and dependencies

The argument works for every finite `omega` and needs no symmetry or derivative decay. It fixes only pressure modulo constants. It does not infer pointwise `P->constant`, `U in L^2`, a rate of velocity decay, or vanishing pressure flux for a general bounded decaying infinite-energy profile.

The functional-analytic ingredients are proved or specified above: the integrable action of a double Riesz transform on zero-mean Schwartz tests, tempered harmonic distributions being polynomials, and the elementary `L^2` multiplier bound. No recent Liouville theorem is used. No numerical computation or formal verification is involved.
